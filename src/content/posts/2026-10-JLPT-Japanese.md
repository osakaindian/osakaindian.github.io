There are thousands of Japanese resources out there, and most guides dump all of them on you at once. This one works differently: **find your level, open that section, and ignore the rest** until you need it.

## Not sure of your level?

| If this sounds like you... | Start here |
|---|---|
| I can't read hiragana or katakana yet | **Level 0** |
| I know the basics and can introduce myself, order food, ask simple questions | **Level 1** |
| I can have simple daily conversations but struggle with news, forms, or fast speech | **Level 2** |
| I can follow most daily life and study, but want to sound natural or handle work and academic Japanese | **Level 3** |

*The levels roughly match JLPT: Level 0 is before N5, Level 1 is N5 to N4, Level 2 is N3, and Level 3 is N2 and above. You don't need to take the JLPT to use this guide.*

## Pick your level

<details>
<summary><strong>Level 0: Complete beginner</strong></summary>

**Goal for the first month:** read hiragana and katakana, and survive daily situations (greetings, shops, restaurants, trains).

**Focus on**

- Hiragana, then katakana. Most people manage each in one to two weeks of short daily practice.
- 50 to 100 survival phrases, not grammar theory.
- Listening to the sounds of Japanese. The pronunciation is simpler than most languages.

**Resources**

- **Textbook:** Genki I or Minna no Nihongo I. Pick one and stick with it.
- **Free courses:** The Japan Foundation's *Irodori* and *Marugoto* online courses are designed for people living in Japan.
- **Apps:** Any kana trainer (Tofugu's guides are popular), plus Anki or a similar flashcard app.
- **In person:** Ask your university or local international association about free beginner classes. Many are run by volunteers.

**Advice**

- Don't start with kanji yet. Kana first, then kanji slowly alongside vocabulary.
- Use what you learn the same day: say "arigatou gozaimasu" at the convenience store, read the signs on your commute.

**Common mistake:** relying on romaji for too long. It feels easier now, but it slows you down badly later.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Level 1: Beginner (N5 to N4)</strong></summary>

**Goal:** hold simple conversations, read short texts with furigana, and learn your first 300 to 500 kanji over time.

**Focus on**

- Core grammar: particles, verb forms (て-form is the big one), simple past and negative.
- Vocabulary from your real life: campus, shopping, transport, hobbies.
- Speaking from the start, even if it's imperfect.

**Resources**

- **Textbook:** Finish Genki I and II (or Minna no Nihongo I and II). A grammar reference like *Tae Kim's Guide* or *Bunpro* helps when something doesn't click.
- **Kanji and vocab:** WaniKani, Anki, or a textbook-based deck. Choose one system, not three.
- **Reading:** Graded readers (Tadoku series) and simple news with furigana.
- **Listening:** *Nihongo con Teppei* (beginner podcast) and *Comprehensible Japanese* on YouTube.
- **Speaking:** Language exchange events, conversation cafés, or tutoring (italki and similar platforms).

**Advice**

- Aim for 20 to 30 minutes a day rather than 3 hours on Sunday. Consistency beats intensity.
- Make one Japanese friend or language partner. It changes everything.

**Common mistake:** studying only from the textbook and never using Japanese with real people.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Level 2: Lower intermediate (N3)</strong></summary>

**Goal:** understand everyday Japanese at normal speed and read real material (menus, notices, simple articles).

This is the level where many people feel stuck. The textbook becomes less useful, and real Japanese feels too fast and too full of unknown words. That's normal.

**Focus on**

- Building vocabulary and kanji faster, now that you have a base.
- Reading and listening in large amounts, rather than studying grammar lists.
- Grammar that links ideas: reasons, conditions, opinions, explanations.

**Resources**

- **Textbook:** *Tobira* or *Quartet* for structured study. *Soumatome* or *Shin Kanzen Master* if you're aiming for JLPT.
- **Reading:** Graded readers at higher levels, *Satori Reader*, easy news sites, and manga with furigana.
- **Listening:** Japanese YouTube channels on topics you already enjoy, plus anime or dramas with Japanese subtitles.
- **Dictionaries:** Jisho.org and a good dictionary app that lets you look up kanji by drawing.
- **In person:** University Japanese classes, volunteer classes, and part-time jobs where you actually use Japanese.

**Advice**

- Pick content you *like*. You'll read ten pages of a manga you love, but not two pages of a textbook.
- Don't look up every unknown word. Guess from context, and only look up words that keep appearing.
- Consider setting a JLPT date as a deadline if you work well with goals.

**Common mistake:** staying in an English-speaking bubble because it's comfortable. Intermediate learners improve fastest by changing their daily environment, even slightly.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Level 3: Upper intermediate and advanced (N2 and above)</strong></summary>

**Goal:** use Japanese for academic work, job hunting, and natural conversation with different levels of politeness.

**Focus on**

- **Register:** knowing when to use casual, polite, and honorific Japanese (keigo), especially for part-time jobs, internships, and job hunting.
- **Academic and professional vocabulary** in your own field.
- **Natural expression:** the difference between "correct" and "what a native speaker would actually say."

**Resources**

- **JLPT prep:** *Shin Kanzen Master* N2 and N1 series are standard.
- **Reading:** Newspapers, novels, essays, and articles in your own field. Start with short pieces and build up.
- **Writing and speaking:** Ask a Japanese friend or tutor to correct your emails, reports, and self-introductions. Corrected output teaches more than extra input at this stage.
- **Career:** Your university's career center often offers keigo and interview practice for international students.

**Advice**

- Progress slows down and becomes harder to measure. Track it by what you can *do* (read a paper, join a seminar discussion, write an email) rather than by test scores.
- Listen to how people around you speak. Real spoken Japanese differs a lot from textbooks.

**Common mistake:** treating the N1 as the finish line. Passing the test and working comfortably in Japanese are related but different goals.

</details>

## Advice that applies at every level

- **Choose fewer resources.** One textbook, one vocabulary system, one listening source. Switching constantly is the most common way to waste months.
- **Make mistakes early and often.** Japanese people are generally patient and happy when you try, even if it's rough.
- **Use Japanese in your actual life.** Order in Japanese, text your friends in Japanese, change your phone's language. Small habits add up.
- **Study a little every day.** Fifteen minutes daily is better than one long weekly session.
- **Don't compare yourself to others.** Everyone's pace depends on their time, background, and how much Japanese they hear in daily life.

## Our opinion

These are the committee's views, not rules, so take them as you like.

- **The JLPT is useful, but it isn't the goal.** It's a good checkpoint and often required for jobs or scholarships, but it doesn't test speaking. Don't let it replace actually talking to people.
- **Apps are a supplement.** Streaks and points feel productive, but real progress comes from reading, listening, and using the language with people.
- **Don't put off speaking until you feel ready.** You won't ever feel ready. Start now.
- **Living here is a huge advantage.** Take it. Join a club, a volunteer group, or a part-time job where Japanese is the default.
